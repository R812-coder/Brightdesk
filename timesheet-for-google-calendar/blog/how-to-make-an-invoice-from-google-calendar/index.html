The calendar-to-invoice flow

Most freelancers treat calendar management and invoicing as two separate activities. But they’re the same data in different formats. Your calendar event “Acme Corp — Design Review, 10:00-11:30” is the same information as the invoice line item “Design Review: 1.5 hours at $150 = $225.”

The flow is simple: Calendar events → Timesheet (hours by project) → Invoice (hours × rate = amount). Let’s walk through each step.

Step 1: Generate your timesheet

You need a list of events with dates, durations, and project categories. Two options:

Manual

Open your calendar and spreadsheet side by side. For each event in the billing period, record the date, hours, and description. This takes 20-30 minutes for a typical month.

Automated

Use Timesheet for Google Calendar to generate the timesheet in one click. It reads your events, categorizes by color, calculates hours with rounding, and outputs a formatted spreadsheet with a Summary tab showing totals per project.

Step 2: Review for errors

Before building your invoice, check for:

  • Uncategorized events — Billable work you forgot to color or categorize
  • Overlapping events — Two events at the same time could mean double-billing
  • Suspiciously short events — A 3-minute event is probably not billable
  • Missing days — Did you work on Tuesday but have no events? Add them before invoicing

If you use the add-on, the Review tab flags all of these automatically. If you’re working manually, scan through your timesheet for anything that looks off.

Step 3: Build the invoice

Take the per-project totals from your timesheet and plug them into your invoice template:

  • Line items: Each project gets a row with description, hours, rate, and amount
  • Supporting detail: Optionally attach the full timesheet as a PDF for clients who want to see individual entries
  • Total: Sum of all line items

Free invoice template

If you don’t have an invoice template, create one in Google Docs or Google Sheets with these sections:

  1. Header: Your business name, address, email, logo
  2. Client info: Client name, address, contact
  3. Invoice details: Invoice number, date, due date, payment terms
  4. Line items: Description, hours, rate, amount (pulled from your timesheet summary)
  5. Total: Sum with any applicable taxes
  6. Payment instructions: Bank details, PayPal, Stripe, etc.

Save it as a template and duplicate it each billing period. The only part that changes each month is the line items — which come directly from your timesheet.

Tips for professional invoices

Use consistent invoice numbers

INV-2026-001, INV-2026-002, etc. Clients and accountants need these for record keeping.

Include the billing period

“Services rendered: March 1-31, 2026.” This matches the date range in your timesheet.

Attach the detailed timesheet

Some clients want to see exactly which meetings and work sessions they’re paying for. Export your Timesheet tab as a PDF and attach it. This builds trust and reduces pushback on hours.

Set clear payment terms

“Net 30” or “Due within 14 days.” Include late payment terms if applicable.

Round professionally

Bill in quarter-hour (0.25) increments, not to the minute. “24.50 hours” reads cleaner than “24.4167 hours.”

Automate the entire process

Here’s the 60-second billing workflow once your system is set up:

  1. Open your timesheet spreadsheet
  2. Extensions → Timesheet for Google Calendar → Refresh Timesheet (one click)
  3. Check the Review tab for any flagged issues
  4. Open the Summary tab — copy project totals into your invoice template
  5. Send the invoice

Total time: under 60 seconds for steps 1-4. The entire calendar-to-invoice pipeline, from raw events to sent invoice, takes less time than filling out a single Harvest time entry.
