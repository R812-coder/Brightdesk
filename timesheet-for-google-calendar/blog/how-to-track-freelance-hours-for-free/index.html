What freelancers actually spend on time tracking

The irony of time tracking is that it takes time. If you bill at $100/hour and spend 10 minutes per day on time entry, that’s $33 of your time per week — $1,700 per year — spent on the administrative act of recording your work. Add a $12/month subscription and you’re investing $1,850 per year in knowing how many hours you worked.

There’s a better way. The best free time tracking method is the one that doesn’t feel like time tracking at all.

Method 1: Manual spreadsheet (free, tedious)

The most common starting point: a Google Sheet with columns for Date, Client, Task, Start, End, and Hours. You fill it in at the end of each day.

Pros: Completely free, you own the data, no software to learn. Cons: Relies on memory (inaccurate by end of day), takes 10-15 minutes daily, easy to skip on busy days, and when you skip a day you can’t reconstruct it.

Method 2: Free-tier time tracking apps

Tools like Toggl, Clockify, and Harvest offer free tiers. They’re real time trackers with start/stop buttons, dashboards, and reports.

Pros: Dedicated interface, automatic duration calculation, reports. Cons: You must remember to start and stop timers (most freelancers forget 2-3 times per week), free tiers have limits (Toggl free: no project rates, Harvest free: 1 project only), and you’re entering data into a separate system from your calendar.

The forgotten-timer problem: Studies of time tracking app usage show that freelancers forget to start or stop timers an average of 3 times per week. Each forgotten timer means either lost billable hours or inaccurate invoices. A tool that requires you to remember to press a button has a built-in failure mode.

Method 3: Google Calendar (free, automatic)

The approach that eliminates the forgotten-timer problem entirely: use your Google Calendar as your time tracker. You already block time for client work. Those blocks are your time entries.

How it works: color-code events by client (one click when creating an event), then at billing time, extract the data into a spreadsheet. The events were created to manage your schedule, not to track time — but they contain all the same data a time tracker would.

To extract the data, use Timesheet for Google Calendar (free 7-day trial, then $49/year) or export manually. The add-on maps your calendar colors to client names and hourly rates, then generates a formatted timesheet in one click.

5-minute setup for the calendar method

  1. Decide on colors (1 min) — Tomato for Client A, Basil for Client B, Peacock for Client C. Write it on a sticky note.
  2. Color your existing events (2 min) — Go through this week’s events and assign colors to client work.
  3. Install the timesheet add-on (1 min) — Open any Google Sheet, Extensions → Add-ons → Get add-ons → Timesheet for Google Calendar.
  4. Map colors to clients (1 min) — In the sidebar, assign each color to a client name and rate.

Done. From now on, just color events as you create them. That’s the entire system.

How many hours freelancers actually lose

A study by Accelo found that professionals who don’t track time in real-time lose an average of 15-20% of their billable hours. For a freelancer billing 30 hours per week at $100/hour, that’s $150-$200 per week in lost revenue — over $8,000 per year.

The calendar method minimizes this because the time entries are created proactively (when you schedule the work) rather than reconstructed from memory (at the end of the day). You can’t forget to log a meeting that’s already on your calendar.

The bottom line: The best free time tracking system is the one that requires zero extra effort. Your Google Calendar already tracks your time passively. You just need to format the output.
