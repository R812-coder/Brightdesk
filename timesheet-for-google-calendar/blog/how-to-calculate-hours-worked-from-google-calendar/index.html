How to Calculate Hours Worked from Google Calendar

Your Google Calendar already knows when you started, when you stopped, and what you worked on. Here’s how to turn that into hours worked — manually or automatically.

In this guide
  1. What data Google Calendar already has
  2. Calculate hours manually in a spreadsheet
  3. How to round time for billing

  6. Build a weekly hours summary

What data Google Calendar already has

Every timed event in Google Calendar has four things a timesheet needs:

  • Date — When the work happened
  • Start time — When you started
  • End time — When you stopped
  • Title — What the work was

From start and end time, you can calculate duration. From the title (or event color), you can categorize by project. The only thing you need to add is the hourly rate, which is constant per client.

Calculate hours manually in a spreadsheet

If you’re doing this by hand, set up a Google Sheet with these columns:

  • Column A: Date (e.g., 3/24/2026)
  • Column B: Start (e.g., 9:00 AM)
  • Column C: End (e.g., 10:30 AM)
  • Column D: Description
  • Column E: Hours — formula: =(C2-B2)*24

The formula =(C2-B2)*24 works because Google Sheets stores times as fractions of a day. Multiplying by 24 converts to hours. A 90-minute meeting (9:00 to 10:30) returns 1.5.

For a running total at the bottom, use =SUM(E2:E100).

How to round time for billing

Most billing practices don’t use exact times. The three standard rounding methods:

Quarter-hour rounding (15 minutes)

The most common method. 22 minutes rounds to 0.25 hours (15 min). 38 minutes rounds to 0.50 hours (30 min). Formula: =CEILING(E2*4,1)/4

Tenth-hour rounding (6 minutes)

Common in legal billing. 8 minutes rounds to 0.2 hours (12 min). Formula: =CEILING(E2*10,1)/10

Exact time

No rounding. A 47-minute meeting is billed as 0.783 hours. Less common but used in some contracts.

Always round up, not to the nearest increment. Industry standard is to round up to the next billing increment. 16 minutes rounds to 30 minutes (0.50 hours), not to 15 minutes (0.25 hours). Use CEILING, not ROUND.

Edge cases that break manual calculations

If you’re calculating hours by hand, watch out for these:

Events spanning midnight

A late-night work session from 11:00 PM to 1:00 AM shows negative hours with =(C2-B2)*24 because the end time is “before” the start time in the same-day formula. Fix: =IF(C2>B2,(C2-B2)*24,(1-B2+C2)*24)

Recurring events

Your weekly team standup shows as one event in Calendar but represents 4-5 separate time entries per month. Manual extraction requires you to create a row for each occurrence.

Declined meetings

Events you declined still appear in your calendar. If you extract all events without filtering, you’ll overcount your hours.

All-day events

Holidays, reminders, and milestones are all-day events with no start/end time. Including them in your hours calculation produces errors.

Build a weekly hours summary

For a quick weekly summary by project, add a Project column (Column F) and use a pivot table or SUMIF:

=SUMIF(F:F, "Client A", E:E)

This sums all hours where the project is “Client A.” Add one formula per project and you have a summary view.

Or, if you use the Timesheet for Google Calendar add-on, the Summary tab does this automatically — total hours and total billing per project, with a grand total at the bottom.
