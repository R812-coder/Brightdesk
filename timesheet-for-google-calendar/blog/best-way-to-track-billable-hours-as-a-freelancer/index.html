Best Way to Track Billable Hours as a Freelancer

After testing manual spreadsheets, Toggl, Harvest, Clockify, and the Google Calendar method, here’s what actually works — ranked by accuracy, effort, and cost.

What makes a tracking system “best”?

Not all time tracking is equal. The best system optimizes for three things:

  • Accuracy — Does it capture all billable work, including ad-hoc calls and unscheduled tasks?
  • Effort — How much daily overhead does it add? Every minute spent tracking is a minute not billing.
  • Reliability — Does it work even when you forget? Systems that rely on human memory have a built-in failure rate.

5 methods ranked

5. End-of-day memory reconstruction (worst)

Sitting down at 6 PM and trying to remember what you did at 10 AM. Research shows humans overestimate focus time and underestimate breaks. This method typically misses 15-20% of billable hours.

4. Manual spreadsheet

Better than memory because you log throughout the day, but requires discipline. Most freelancers abandon it within 2 weeks because it’s tedious.

3. Start/stop timer apps (Toggl, Clockify)

Accurate when you remember to use them. The problem: you forget to start the timer 2-3 times per week, and when you forget to stop it, you get inflated hours. Free tiers have limited features.

2. Harvest (paid)

Reliable, well-designed, good reports. But $12/month for a solo freelancer is steep when the output is the same data your calendar already has. And you’re still entering data manually.

1. Google Calendar + automated extraction (best)

Your calendar events are created to manage your schedule — time tracking is a free side effect. No timer to start or stop. No separate app. No data duplication. And with an add-on, extraction takes one click.

The winner (and why)

The Google Calendar method wins because it’s the only system with zero daily effort. You don’t track time — your calendar does it passively. The events exist because you scheduled your work, not because you remembered to log it.

The one caveat: unscheduled work (a quick client call, an email chain that turns into an hour of research) won’t appear unless you create a calendar event after the fact. This is the one discipline you need. But adding a 15-minute event takes 10 seconds, versus the 5-10 minutes daily overhead of a dedicated time tracker.

The 3 biggest tracking mistakes freelancers make

1. Not tracking at all

Some freelancers quote flat rates specifically to avoid time tracking. But even with flat rates, knowing your actual hours tells you whether the project was profitable. If you quoted $5,000 and spent 80 hours, your effective rate was $62.50/hr — and you need to know that for future quotes.

2. Tracking selectively

Only logging “big” work blocks and ignoring 15-minute calls, email responses, and quick revisions. These add up to 3-5 hours per week that disappear from your records.

3. Not reviewing before invoicing

Sending an invoice without checking for overlaps, uncategorized time, or missing days. One overlap error on a $150/hr engagement is a $150 overbill that damages client trust when caught.

Choose the right rounding method

Most client contracts specify a rounding method. The three standards:

  • Quarter-hour (15 min) — Most common. 22 minutes rounds up to 0.25 hours. Clean for invoicing.
  • Tenth-hour (6 min) — Used in legal billing. 8 minutes rounds to 0.2 hours. More granular.
  • Exact — 47 minutes = 0.783 hours. Most precise but looks odd on invoices.

When in doubt, use quarter-hour. It’s the industry standard for consulting and most clients expect it.

The weekly billing workflow

Here’s the system that takes under 2 minutes per week:

  1. During the week: Use Google Calendar normally. Color events by client. Add unscheduled work as events after the fact.
  2. Friday (60 seconds): Open your timesheet spreadsheet. Extensions → Timesheet for Google Calendar → Refresh Timesheet.
  3. Friday (30 seconds): Check the Review tab for flagged issues. Fix any uncategorized events.
  4. Friday (30 seconds): Check the Summary tab. Copy totals into your invoice if it’s billing day.

That’s 2 minutes per week. Compare that to 50-75 minutes per week with manual time entry. Over a year, the calendar method saves you 40-60 hours — an entire work week — of administrative time.

The real test of a time tracking system: If you can go on vacation for a week, come back, and reconstruct your pre-vacation timesheet accurately, your system works. With the calendar method, every event is already recorded. With a start/stop timer, you’d have nothing.
